# 1. awkward

awkward
“There was an awkward silence after his comment.”
What does this expression mean?
Meaning:
Uncomfortable, embarrassing, or difficult in a social situation.
Category:
Adjective
Pronunciation tip:
Native English:
/ˈɔkwɚd/ — the stress is on the first syllable. The second syllable is weak and reduced.
For Spanish speakers:
Keep the second syllable weak. Use an American r without tapping or trilling it as in Spanish. Do not add a vowel after the final d.
More examples:
- I always feel awkward when I don’t know anyone at a party.
- The conversation got awkward when they started arguing.
- That was an awkward moment, but everyone eventually laughed about it.
- She’s gotten less awkward in meetings as she’s become more confident.
Common collocations/chunks:
- awkward silence
- awkward moment
- feel awkward
- socially awkward
- get awkward
- kind of awkward
Common mistake and usage note:
Awkward describes a situation or person that feels uncomfortable or socially difficult.
✅ “I felt awkward.”
✅ “It was an awkward conversation.”
Do not confuse awkward with embarrassed.
Awkward describes the uncomfortable situation or feeling.
Embarrassed specifically means feeling ashamed or self-conscious.
Natural alternatives:
- uncomfortable
- embarrassing
- weird, in informal contexts
Spanish Translation:
incómodo / vergonzoso / torpe socialmente, depending on context

# 2. draining

draining
“Long meetings can be mentally draining.”
What does this expression mean?
Meaning:
Making you feel very tired, emotionally exhausted, or low on energy.
Category:
Adjective — present participle form of drain used as an adjective
Pronunciation tip:
Native English:
/ˈdreɪnɪŋ/ — the stress is on the first syllable. The ending -ing is /ɪŋ/, not /in/.
For Spanish speakers:
The vowel /ɪ/ in -ing differs in quality from Spanish /i/, not just in length. End with the nasal /ŋ/ at the back of the mouth, without a separate hard g sound.
More examples:
- Dealing with constant complaints is emotionally draining.
- Yesterday’s meeting was really draining.
- This project has been draining lately, but we’re almost finished.
- Working long hours without breaks can become physically and mentally draining.
Common collocations/chunks:
- emotionally draining
- mentally draining
- physically draining
- really draining
- emotionally and mentally draining
- find something draining
Common mistake and usage note:
Draining describes the thing that causes tiredness.
✅ “The meeting was draining.”
Drained describes how a person feels afterward.
✅ “I felt drained after the meeting.”
❌ “I felt draining after the meeting.”
Natural alternatives:
- exhausting
- tiring
- emotionally exhausting
- wearing
Spanish Translation:
agotador / desgastante / que te deja sin energía

# 3. went

went
“I went straight home after work.”
What does this expression mean?
Meaning:
Went is the past simple form of go. It means that someone moved, traveled, or proceeded somewhere in the past.
Category:
Verb — past simple form of go
Irregular Forms:
go → went → gone
Pronunciation tip:
Native English:
/wɛnt/ — one syllable. The vowel is /ɛ/, like the vowel in bed.
The irregular forms sound very different:
- go /ɡoʊ/
- went /wɛnt/
- gone /ɡɔn/
For Spanish speakers:
Start with /w/ without adding a g before it. The vowel /ɛ/ is more open than Spanish /e/. Keep the final /nt/ cluster and do not add a vowel after t.
More examples:
- I usually go to the gym after work.
- We went out for dinner last night.
- She’s already gone home.
- He goes to that coffee shop almost every morning.
Common collocations/chunks:
- went home
- went out
- went back
- went to work
- went wrong
- went well
Common mistake and usage note:
Use went for the past simple: “I went there yesterday.” After have/has/had, use a past participle: “She has gone home.” Do not say “She has went home.” For completed visits, been is often clearer: “I’ve been there several times.” Went, gone, and been are not interchangeable.
Natural alternatives:
- traveled, in some contexts
- headed
- left, depending on context
- moved, depending on context
Spanish Translation:
fui / fue / fueron / iba, depending on context

# 4. put off

put off
“I keep putting off going to the dentist.”
What does this expression mean?
Meaning:
To delay doing something until a later time, often because you don’t want to do it yet.
Category:
Phrasal verb
Irregular Forms:
put → put → put
Pronunciation tip:
Native English:
/pʊt ɔf/ — the base, past, and past participle forms of put are pronounced the same. In connected speech, link put and off without inserting an extra vowel.
For Spanish speakers:
Keep /ʊ/ relaxed; it differs in quality from Spanish /u/. Link put and off without inserting an extra vowel, and finish off with /f/.
More examples:
- I usually put off difficult tasks until the last minute.
- I put off calling him because I didn’t know what to say.
- We’ve put off the meeting until next week.
- Don’t keep putting it off—you’ll have to do it eventually.
Common collocations/chunks:
- put something off
- put it off
- put off doing something
- keep putting something off
- put off a meeting
- put off until later
Common mistake and usage note:
When put off is followed by another action, use an -ing form, not an infinitive.
✅ “I put off calling him.”
❌ “I put off to call him.”
Because put is irregular but does not change form:
“I put off the appointment yesterday.”
“We’ve put off the meeting twice.”
Natural alternatives:
- postpone
- delay
- push back
- leave until later
Spanish Translation:
posponer / dejar para después / aplazar

# 5. fuel

fuel
“Seeing progress fuels my motivation.”
What does this expression mean?
Meaning:
In this context, fuel means to provide energy, motivation, or support that makes something stronger or keeps it growing.
Category:
Verb
Pronunciation tip:
Native English:
/ˈfjuːəl/ — usually pronounced with two quick syllables. The first syllable contains /fjuː/, similar to few.
For Spanish speakers:
Move smoothly from /f/ into the /j/ sound, like the y in yes. Keep the weak second vowel /ə/ short and relaxed; do not turn it into a strong open vowel.
More examples:
- Positive feedback often fuels my motivation.
- His criticism fueled her determination to prove him wrong.
- The recent success has fueled even more interest in the project.
- Constant rumors can fuel unnecessary conflict at work.
Common collocations/chunks:
- fuel motivation
- fuel growth
- fuel interest
- fuel a debate
- fuel conflict
- fuel speculation
Common mistake and usage note:
Fuel can be both a noun and a verb, but on this card it is used as a verb.
As a noun:
“We need more fuel for the car.”
As a verb:
“Competition fuels innovation.”
Regular past form:
“The announcement fueled speculation.”
In American English, fueled is usually spelled with one l.
Natural alternatives:
- drive
- motivate
- encourage
- feed, in some figurative contexts
Spanish Translation:
impulsar / alimentar / fomentar / dar energía a
