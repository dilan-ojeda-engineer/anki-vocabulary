# 1. AWKWARD

## FRONT

**awkward**

“I felt awkward asking for help.”

**What does this expression mean?**

## BACK

**Meaning:**

Feeling uneasy or self‑conscious in a social situation.

**Category:**

adjective

**Pronunciation tip:**

**Native English:**

General American IPA: /ˈɔːkwɚd/. Stress on the first syllable; the /kw/ cluster is pronounced like the 'qu' in 'quick', and the final /d/ is clearly voiced.

**For Spanish speakers:**

For Spanish speakers: start with an open 'o' similar to 'ó' in 'cómodo', keep the 'kw' sound (practice saying 'cu' quickly), and finish with a soft, voiced 'd' – avoid dropping the final consonant.

**More examples:**

- She laughed, but I could tell she felt awkward after the joke fell flat.
- He gave an awkward smile when his boss called his name by mistake.
- During the interview, the candidate seemed awkward when asked about gaps in the resume.
- The new software made the workflow feel awkward until we got used to it.

**Common collocations/chunks:**

- feel awkward about
- awkward silence
- awkward moment
- awkwardly placed
- awkwardly worded

**Common mistake and usage note:**

Common mistake: using 'awkward' as a noun (e.g., \*'He is an awkward'\*). It is an adjective; you need a noun after it, such as \*'awkward person'\* or \*'awkward situation'\*. Example: \*'He felt awkward in the crowd'\* is correct, but \*'He felt an awkward'\* is not.

**Natural alternatives:**

- uncomfortable
- self‑conscious
- ill at ease
- clumsy

**Spanish Translation:**

# 2. DRAINING

## FRONT

**draining**

“Back-to-back meetings can be draining.”

**What does this expression mean?**

## BACK

**Meaning:**

Very tiring or exhausting; it takes a lot of energy or enthusiasm away.

**Category:**

adjective

**Pronunciation tip:**

**Native English:**

/ˈdreɪ.nɪŋ/ – stress on the first syllable, the diphthong /eɪ/ as in “day”, and a clear /ŋ/ at the end.

**For Spanish speakers:**

Say the first syllable like the English “day” (not the Spanish “de”), keep the /r/ soft, and finish with the nasal /ŋ/ (like the “ng” in “canción”). The final “‑ing” is pronounced as a single sound, not as separate “i‑ng”.

**More examples:**

- The long commute after work was really draining.
- She found the constant noise in the open office to be draining.
- After three hours of intense training, the athletes felt draining.
- Dealing with the endless paperwork can become draining over time.

**Common collocations/chunks:**

- emotionally draining
- draining experience
- draining job
- draining schedule
- draining conversation

**Common mistake and usage note:**

Use “draining” for tasks, situations, or interactions that sap energy. It is not used for literal liquids; for that you would say “leaking” or “dripping”. Example mistake: \*The faucet is draining\* (incorrect for a leaky faucet).

**Natural alternatives:**

- exhausting
- tiring
- fatiguing
- wearing out

**Spanish Translation:**

agotador

# 3. WENT

## FRONT

**went**

“I went to the office yesterday.”

**What does this expression mean?**

## BACK

**Meaning:**

past tense of go; it means that the speaker traveled or moved to a place in the past

**Category:**

verb

**Irregular Forms:**

go → went → gone

**Pronunciation tip:**

**Native English:**

/wɛnt/ – the vowel is the short e as in “bet”, and the final /t/ is clearly released; stress is on the only syllable

**For Spanish speakers:**

Make sure the vowel sounds like the English short e, not the Spanish /e/; keep the final /t/ unvoiced – avoid turning it into a soft /d/ that Spanish speakers often do

**More examples:**

- She went home after the meeting ended.
- They went abroad for their summer vacation.
- He went through the report twice before signing it.
- We went out for coffee during the break.

**Common collocations/chunks:**

- go / went to (a place)
- went through (something)
- went out (for a activity)
- went over (details)
- went ahead (with a plan)
- went missing (disappeared)

**Common mistake and usage note:**

Learners often try to use “went” with a perfect aspect (e.g., \*"I have went"), but the correct form is “I have gone”. Use “went” only for simple past actions.

**Natural alternatives:**

- made my way to
- arrived at

# 4. PUT OFF

## FRONT

**put off**

“Let's not put off this conversation.”

**What does this expression mean?**

## BACK

**Meaning:**

to delay or postpone something, especially a discussion, meeting, or task

**Category:**

phrasal verb

**Pronunciation tip:**

**Native English:**

IPA: /pʊt ɔf/. Stress on the first word. The /t/ is released clearly; the vowel in "off" is the open-mid back rounded vowel, not a short "o" as in "pot".

**For Spanish speakers:**

Focus on the /t/ at the end of "put" – make it a crisp, unreleased /t/ as in Spanish "t" but without a following vowel. The "off" starts with a short /ɒ/ sound, which is more open than the Spanish "o"; try to keep the lips slightly rounded and the throat relaxed.

**More examples:**

- We should put off the deadline until next Friday.
- She kept putting off her dentist appointment for months.
- The manager decided to put off the budget review because of the holiday rush.
- Don't put off studying for the exam; start today.

**Common collocations/chunks:**

- put off the meeting
- put off until later
- put off for good
- put off by the idea
- put off doing something

**Common mistake and usage note:**

A common mistake is to use "put off" when you mean "postpone" but then add a direct object that doesn't fit, e.g., \*"put off the conversation"\* is fine, but \*"put off the coffee"\* would be wrong unless you mean "discourage". Also, "put off" can mean "to discourage" (e.g., "The loud noise put me off"), so be sure the context is about delaying.

**Natural alternatives:**

- delay
- postpone
- defer
- table

**Spanish Translation:**

posponer esta conversación

# 5. FUEL

## FRONT

**fuel**

“These rumors fuel the argument.”

**What does this expression mean?**

## BACK

**Meaning:**

to make a feeling, situation, or conflict stronger or more intense

**Category:**

verb

**Pronunciation tip:**

**Native English:**

General American IPA: /fjul/. Stress on the single syllable. The initial /f/ is a normal voiceless labiodental fricative, the /j/ glide follows directly, and the final /l/ is a clear alveolar lateral. Link it smoothly to the next word if it begins with a vowel (e.g., fuel up).

**For Spanish speakers:**

For Spanish speakers: start with the /f/ sound as in "fácil", then glide quickly into the /j/ (like the English "y" in "yes"). The vowel is a long /u/ similar to the Spanish "u" in "luna" but held a bit longer. Finish with a crisp /l/; avoid adding a vowel after it. Practice the cluster /fj/ together, not as separate sounds.

**More examples:**

- The scandal fueled the public's anger toward the company.
- His passionate speech fueled the team's motivation before the game.
- Social media posts have been fueling the debate about privacy rights.
- The new evidence fueled the investigators' determination to solve the case.

**Common collocations/chunks:**

- fuel the fire
- fuel speculation
- fuel growth
- fuel the debate
- fuel tension

**Common mistake and usage note:**

Fuel is a transitive verb; it needs an object (what is being intensified). Learners often try to use it intransitively, e.g., \*"The rumors fuel"\* which is incorrect. Use it with a noun that can be intensified, such as "argument," "anger," or "interest".

**Natural alternatives:**

- stoke
- intensify
- aggravate
- exacerbate

**Spanish Translation:**

Estos rumores alimentan la discusión.
