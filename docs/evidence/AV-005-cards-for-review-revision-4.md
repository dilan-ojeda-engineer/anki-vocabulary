# 1. AWKWARD

## FRONT

**awkward**

“I felt awkward asking for help.”

**What does this expression mean?**

## BACK

**Meaning:**

Feeling uncomfortable or self-conscious in a social situation, especially when you are unsure what to say or do.

**Category:**

Adjective

**Pronunciation tip:**

**Native English:**

/ˈɑːkwɚd/ or /ˈɔːkwɚd/. Stress the first syllable. Keep the second syllable weak, with an American r-colored vowel.

**For Spanish speakers:**

Keep the second syllable weak. Use an American r without tapping or trilling it as in Spanish. Do not add a vowel after the final d.

**More examples:**

- I always feel awkward when I don’t know anyone at a party.
- The conversation got awkward when they started arguing.
- That was an awkward moment, but everyone eventually laughed about it.
- She’s gotten less awkward in meetings as she’s become more confident.

**Common collocations/chunks:**

- feel awkward
- an awkward silence
- an awkward moment
- socially awkward
- get awkward
- kind of awkward

**Common mistake and usage note:**

Use feel awkward for social discomfort: I felt awkward asking for help. Embarrassed is more specific: you feel self-conscious about something, often something you did. Awkward can also describe something difficult to use or handle; it is not limited to social situations.

**Natural alternatives:**

- uncomfortable
- uneasy
- self-conscious

# 2. DRAINING

## FRONT

**draining**

“Back-to-back meetings can be draining.”

**What does this expression mean?**

## BACK

**Meaning:**

Making you feel very tired or low on energy, especially mentally or emotionally.

**Category:**

Adjective — the -ing form of the regular verb drain used as an adjective

**Pronunciation tip:**

**Native English:**

/ˈdreɪnɪŋ/. Stress the first syllable. In careful pronunciation, -ing ends in /ɪŋ/: keep the nasal /ŋ/ without adding a separate g sound.

**For Spanish speakers:**

The vowel /ɪ/ in -ing differs in quality from Spanish /i/, not just in length. End with the nasal /ŋ/ at the back of the mouth, without a separate hard g sound.

**More examples:**

- Dealing with constant complaints is emotionally draining.
- Yesterday’s meeting was really draining.
- This project has been draining lately, but we’re almost finished.
- Working long hours without breaks can be physically and mentally draining.

**Common collocations/chunks:**

- emotionally draining
- mentally draining
- physically draining
- really draining
- find something draining
- a draining day

**Common mistake and usage note:**

Draining describes what causes the tiredness: The meeting was draining. Drained describes the person’s feeling: I felt drained after the meeting. Say I feel drained, not I feel draining, when you mean that you are exhausted.

**Natural alternatives:**

- exhausting
- tiring
- wearing me out
- taking a lot out of me

# 3. WENT

## FRONT

**went**

“I went to the office yesterday.”

**What does this expression mean?**

## BACK

**Meaning:**

Went is the past simple of go. Here, it means that I traveled to the office yesterday.

**Category:**

Verb — past simple of go

**Irregular Forms:**

go → went → gone

**Pronunciation tip:**

**Native English:**

/wɛnt/, one syllable; /ɛ/ is the vowel in bed. The forms sound different: go /ɡoʊ/, went /wɛnt/, gone /ɡɔn/ or /ɡɑːn/. Keep the final /nt/ together without adding a vowel; the t may be unreleased.

**For Spanish speakers:**

Start with /w/ without adding a g before it. The vowel /ɛ/ is more open than Spanish /e/. Keep the final /nt/ cluster and do not add a vowel after t.

**More examples:**

- I usually go to the gym after work.
- We went out for dinner last night.
- She’s already gone home.
- He goes to the office early on Mondays.

**Common collocations/chunks:**

- went to work
- went to the office
- went home
- went straight home
- went back
- went out for dinner

**Common mistake and usage note:**

In this example, went describes a completed trip: I went to the office yesterday. After have, has, or had, use gone, not went: She has gone home. After did or didn’t, use go: Did you go to the office?

**Natural alternatives:**

- headed to
- traveled to
- made my way to

# 4. PUT OFF

## FRONT

**put off**

“Let's not put off this conversation.”

**What does this expression mean?**

## BACK

**Meaning:**

To delay an activity or conversation until a later time, sometimes because you do not want to deal with it yet.

**Category:**

Phrasal verb

**Irregular Forms:**

put → put → put

**Pronunciation tip:**

**Native English:**

/pʊt ˈɔf/ or /pʊt ˈɑf/. The base, past, and participle put sound the same. Link the final t into the vowel of off; phrase stress can vary with emphasis.

**For Spanish speakers:**

Keep /ʊ/ relaxed; it differs in quality from Spanish /u/. Link put and off without inserting an extra vowel, and finish off with /f/.

**More examples:**

- I usually put off difficult tasks until the last minute.
- I put off calling him yesterday because I didn’t know what to say.
- We’ve put off the meeting until next week.
- Don’t keep putting it off—you’ll have to do it eventually.

**Common collocations/chunks:**

- put something off
- put it off
- put off doing something
- keep putting something off
- put off a meeting
- put off until later

**Common mistake and usage note:**

For another action, use -ing: put off calling him, not put off to call him. With a pronoun, say put it off, not put off it. With a noun, both put off the meeting and put the meeting off are natural.

**Natural alternatives:**

- postpone
- delay
- push back
- leave it until later

# 5. FUEL

## FRONT

**fuel**

“These rumors fuel the argument.”

**What does this expression mean?**

## BACK

**Meaning:**

To make something stronger or keep it going. Here, the rumors make the argument stronger or harder to stop.

**Category:**

Verb

**Pronunciation tip:**

**Native English:**

/ˈfjuːəl/. The first part sounds like few; move toward the weak vowel /ə/ before the final l. Some American speakers compress the syllables.

**For Spanish speakers:**

Move smoothly from /f/ into the /j/ glide, as in English yes. Avoid replacing that glide with a strongly fricated consonant. Keep the second vowel /ə/ weak rather than turning it into a full open vowel.

**More examples:**

- The new evidence fueled the debate at work.
- His angry comments only fueled the argument.
- Social media posts can fuel rumors and unnecessary conflict.
- The team’s recent success has fueled hopes for a championship.

**Common collocations/chunks:**

- fuel an argument
- fuel a debate
- fuel speculation
- fuel conflict
- fuel someone’s anger
- fuel hopes

**Common mistake and usage note:**

Here, fuel is a verb: rumors fuel an argument by making it stronger. It is regular; the American past spelling is fueled, with one l: The announcement fueled speculation. As a noun, fuel means a substance used for energy: We need more fuel for the car.

**Natural alternatives:**

- feed
- intensify
- add to
- make stronger
